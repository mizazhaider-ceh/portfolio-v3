## 4. Narrative arc

One story, told in order across the site:
- Home, first screen: who he is (one-line role), what he can build, and
  the first proof (PenTrix-VulnLab), with a way to contact him. Gate 1
  is 7 to 30 seconds. Nothing here asks for anything.
- Home, below the fold: the remaining featured projects in audit order,
  then a short About strip, a compact Expertise strip with project
  links, a short Research strip, and Contact. The homepage is a map, not
  the whole territory.
- Projects: the full argument. Three to five featured case studies in
  depth, then a compact archive. Each case study follows the same
  template (problem, role, decisions, build, evidence, what I would
  improve, links), so the visitor learns to trust the pattern.
- About: who he is, where he is, what he is looking for, in short
  verifiable lines, with a timeline that links each item to its evidence.
- Expertise: the same capabilities, grouped by evidence, each group
  linking to the project that proves it.
- Research: direction and ambition, honestly stated, no published output
  claimed.
- Contact: one tap, with an email link that works if JavaScript or a
  form fails.

## 5. Tone and voice rules

Tone: confident, technically credible, professional, direct, human,
distinctive, mature. Short sentences. Concrete nouns. Numbers only with
their proof beside them. The work speaks first.

Banned phrases and patterns, with replacements:
- "I am passionate about technology." Replace with a project and what
  it does.
- "Always learning, always growing." Replace with the newest shipped
  work and its date.
- "I am a beginner looking for an opportunity." Never use. The site
  demonstrates capability; availability is stated once, plainly, in
  About and Contact, as approved wording.
- "Give me a chance." Never use.
- "I am an expert in everything." Never use. Each capability names its
  evidence and its limits.
- Skill percentages, skill bars, and counts of skills. Never use.
- Testimonials, client quotes, employer endorsements. None exist in any
  source. Never invent them.
- "Loved by", "trusted by", "10x", or any unverified superlative.
  Never use.
- Mission statements as the hero. The hero states identity, role, and
  proof, not a philosophy.
- "Self-taught" as a repeated disclaimer. Do not use. The work is the
  credential.

## 6. PenTrix positioning

The PenTrix is presented as an initiative he founded and developed, not
as a course assignment and not as a commercial product. What the site
says about it, from actual evidence only:
- Mission: a deliberately vulnerable learning lab where students learn
  offensive security by exploiting real vulnerability classes in a safe,
  local environment.
- Why it exists: he wanted a lab that teaches the method, not just the
  exploit, with challenges, guidance, and a clear path from finding to
  fixing.
- Technical focus: web vulnerabilities, authentication and authorisation
  flaws, injection, and the surrounding tooling, built as an Express and
  SQLite application with 201 challenges across 29 modules.
- Educational activities: the lab itself, its documentation, and its
  use as a teaching vehicle.
- His responsibilities: founder, lead developer, challenge designer,
  documentation author.
- What the site must not say: that it is deployed publicly (the repo
  states it must not be deployed publicly, so the case study links to
  the repo only), that flags are stored anywhere (only the approved
  sentence: flags are randomly generated per install at first boot, no
  flag literal exists in any tracked file, only genuine exploits earn
  flags), or any user, download, or deployment count that was not
  verified.
